Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in College Park

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which reduces the risk of scalding and slows mineral buildup. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush every year if their water is hard.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters last 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models typically last 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing symptoms like rusty hot water, rumbling during heating, dampness under the tank, or hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, a planned replacement is almost always cheaper and much easier on you than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

Left alone, a small problem tends to grow. Consider a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a short, affordable repair, but once it's gone, the water begins corroding the tank itself, and suddenly a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we encourage homeowners to keep our number in their phone and reach out at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves money and prevents a flooded utility room.

Common Water Heater Problems in College Park

Around College Park, water heater trouble usually trace back to a handful of sources. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be part of the picture: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Age matters just as much. Many homes around here still have a tank that was installed a decade or more ago, and beyond the ten-year mark, the anode rod is usually spent and rust starts working on the steel itself. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Well water can bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a sulfur smell in the hot water usually points to a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older installations often need code updates when a heater is replaced: a thermal expansion tank on closed systems, a properly routed T&P discharge line, drain pans where a leak would hit finished floors, and earthquake straps in areas that require them. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most are much cheaper to address early. If something seems off, a diagnostic visit from our team catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one. Call (443) 405-4339 and we'll take a look.
