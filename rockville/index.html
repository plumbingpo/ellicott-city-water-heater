How Rockville Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

Most early failures start with skipped maintenance, and the habits that prevent them are easy. Flush the tank once a year to clear sediment, get the anode rod checked every few years, and operate the relief valve annually so it doesn't seize. Set the thermostat close to 120 degrees for safer water and less scale. For a tankless heater in a hard-water area, plan on descaling once a year so the heat exchanger stays clean.

How old is your water heater? The rating plate on the tank usually encodes the manufacture date, and its age says a lot about what comes next. Most tank heaters reach 8 to 12 years, heat pump models 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units about 20. If your heater is past that range and is also showing rusty water, rumbling, or dampness under the tank, it's usually due for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and less stressful than an emergency replacement after a leak.

Small problems rarely stay small when they're ignored. Consider a spent anode rod: replacing it is a short, affordable repair, but if nobody replaces it, the water begins corroding the tank steel, and just like that a minor fix has become a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to save our number in their phone and reach out at the first hint of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and spares you a flooded utility room.

Common Water Heater Problems in Rockville

Water heaters in and around Rockville face a predictable set of problems. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, minerals can be a real factor: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Then there's age. When the protective anode rod wears out, usually somewhere in the second half of a tank's life, the tank starts to rust from the inside, and a drip from the bottom seam often shows up next. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Well water may bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water often means a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older homes often need code upgrades when a heater is replaced: a thermal expansion tank on closed systems, a properly routed T&P discharge line, drain pans where a leak could damage finished space, and earthquake straps in areas that require them. All of these is fixable, and most are much cheaper to address early. If something seems off, a quick diagnostic visit can keep a small problem from turning into a flooded floor.
